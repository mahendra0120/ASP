## Overview

A firearm may be generally defined as an assembly of a barrel and action from which a projectile is propelled through the deflagration (rapid burning) of a propellant (gunpowder). As injuries due to firearms are common in most areas of the United States, skill in the interpretation of these injuries is vital for the practitioner of forensic pathology.
There are many unique features of firearms that may be of critical importance in a forensic investigation. Although forensic pathologists need not be experts on all types of firearms, they must be familiar with the basic operation of different weapons in order to interpret the injuries resulting from them. For example, knowledge about the safety features of a weapon that would have allowed or prohibited its operation in a given set of circumstances may be of great importance in determining the plausibility of a given death scene scenario. Similarly, knowledge of muzzle shapes, size and configuration of a weapon, approximate weight of a trigger pull, amount of recoil, and a host of other factors may serve to refute or substantiate a putative explanation for how a firearm injury was sustained.

### Historical information

The history of gunshot wounds necessarily parallels that of the development of firearms themselves. A full treatment of the history of arms development would require volumes, but readers interested in the history of firearms or their function are encouraged to seek more information in the suggested reading sources in the Additional Resources section.
Gunpowder, specifically, black powder, is thought to have originated in China in the late first millennium; by the mid-13th century, evidence of gunpowder formulas and experimentation could be found in European writings. Shortly thereafter, instances of the use of cannons in warfare are reported, and the expansion of the use of the firearm as an instrument of war rapidly followed.
The first firearms were simple tubes of metal, sometimes produced using a hoop-and-stave method, which, by analogy to the design of casks or barrels, gave the gun "barrel" its name. These barrels were closed at the rear of the gun (the breech); loaded from the muzzle by pouring powder into the barrel and tamping down projectile(s) over the powder charge; and discharged by igniting the powder charge through a hole near the breech (the touchhole) using a glowing hot iron or smoldering tinder or match cord. Progressive refinements to these early firearms resulted in a diminution in size, permitting use by a single individual; the addition of a wooden stave or stock to facilitate carrying/aiming the barrel; and the addition of a lock mechanism to permit the discharge of the weapon via a sear lever or trigger. The matchlock was the first such generally successful mechanism; further developments from the 16th to mid-19th centuries included the wheellock and the flintlock.
The coalescence of several major technological developments in the last half of the 19th century — including the percussion cap, metallic cartridge case, and smokeless powder — ultimately permitted the development of the modern breech-loading, repeating firearms of various designs encountered today. Over the course of 500 years, firearms have become more accurate, and rates of fire have progressed from two or three rounds per minute in the days of muzzle-loaders to many hundreds of rounds per minute in modern automatic weaponry. Muzzle velocities of available cartridges have increased greatly, and bullet technology has been steadily refined. In the present day, a wide variety of small arms are available for military and civilian use throughout the world. However, in spite of the dizzying variety of firearms in use, the forensic pathology of gunshot wounds exhibits some consistent patterns. Entrance wounds typically present as circular defects with marginal abrasions, while exit wounds are often more irregular or slit-like. However, atypical wound morphologies occur frequently in practice.

## Modern Ammunition

Most modern ammunition is characterized by a cartridge case that contains the propellant charge and the projectile and is fired by ignition from a shock-sensitive primer.

### The cartridge case, propellant, and primer

Modern cartridges for handguns and rifles are composed of a metallic case, often brass, nickel, or steel, enclosing a powder charge. A bullet, usually lead or copper-encased lead, sits atop the powder charge and is crimped into the case.
At the base of the case is the impact-sensitive primer. Currently, the two types of primer generally encountered will be either centerfire (with a percussion cap-like primer centered within a pocket at the cartridge base, with flash holes to permit entry of the flame to the powder charge) or rimfire (with the priming compound contained within a hollow rim at the base of the casing). The primer is designed to detonate when struck by the firing pin or hammer of a firearm, sending a jet of flame into the overlying powder charge and igniting it.
Gas formed by the burning of the powder charge produces a rapid increase in pressure within the cartridge case and bore of the firearm, forcing the bullet out of the case and down the barrel of the firearm at great speed.
Of particular note in the forensic setting is the fact that the residues of the ignited primer, burning and unburnt powder, and combustion residues can exit the firearm and be deposited on either the shooter or the person shot. The associated findings at autopsy may be of great significance in answering questions regarding the physical aspects of a given shooting incident and are discussed in detail below.

### The projectile

Bullets have a variety of shapes and types of construction depending on their intended use. Traditionally, projectiles have comprised a solid metallic core (usually lead, chosen for its malleability, relatively high density, and relatively low melting point), either with or without a jacket of a gilding metal. Modern commercial ammunition designed for personal defense often has a hollow cavity at the tip (a "hollow point") and a copper jacket surrounding much of the lead bullet. The cavity is designed to facilitate expansion of the bullet in tissue, improving terminal ballistic performance and reducing the possibility of completely perforating the target, potentially causing injury to bystanders. Relatively more recently, proliferations in projectile types (including lightweight, monometallic, segmented, synthetic-coated, and/or frangible bullets) have provided increasingly diverse challenges to the forensic pathologist tasked with their recovery.
Frangible bullets are constructed from compressed metal powders (e.g., copper or iron); these bullets disintegrate upon impact with hard surfaces, minimizing ricochet risks. While advantageous for training and certain operational scenarios, their fragmentation complicates recovery and toolmark analysis.
Modern shotgun ammunition has some similarities to the above, except that the metallic case is replaced by a plastic or (less frequently) cardboard shell, within which is enclosed a number of round shot pellets formed typically of lead, steel, or bismuth. These shot pellets are often enclosed in a plastic "cup" designed to protect and enclose the shot charge on its journey down the barrel of the shotgun, falling away after exiting the weapon. In lieu of a shot cup, some shell designs incorporate a felt and/or plastic disc of "wadding" or "gas seal" over the powder charge, both to protect the shot charge and prevent the excessive escape of gas through and ahead of the shot charge. Shot comes in a variety of sizes, from tiny "birdshot" to much larger "buckshot" pellets. Shotguns are also capable of firing single, large-caliber projectiles known as "slugs."

### Nomenclature

Those unfamiliar with or intimidated by ammunition nomenclature should not feel discouraged; the sometimes esoteric measurements and historical conventions associated with the nomenclature of ammunition can confound even experts. The brief overview presented herein, therefore, is intended only as an introduction to assist the forensic pathologist in beginning to untangle ammunition nomenclature.
The first encounter with ammunition nomenclature typically involves a discussion of caliber, often colloquially taken to mean simply the diameter of the projectile; however, the term can equally be applied to measurements of the bore of the weapon. Furthermore, the caliber may provide merely an approximation of projectile diameter, and when referring to measurements of the bore in rifled weapons, it may denote either the diameter of the bore measured between the grooves or between the lands (raised portions of metal left between the grooves). Beyond the historical issues regarding what measurement is specified by the term caliber, simply knowing the diameter of the projectile or bore does not give a complete picture of the capabilities of the cartridge. As a striking example, both the ubiquitous .22 Long Rifle rimfire cartridge and the military 5.56 × 45 mm centerfire cartridge used in the ArmaLite (AR)-15/model (M)16/M4 family of rifles incorporate different projectiles of approximately .22 inches in diameter.
The most ostensibly rational and accessible ammunition nomenclature is that provided by the metric system, often used for cartridges either developed in Europe or intended for international military use (e.g., the North Atlantic Treaty Organization [NATO]). In this system, the caliber (diameter) of the cartridge is listed first, and the length of the cartridge case is listed second in millimeters (e.g., 9 × 19 mm; 5.56 × 45 mm; 7.62 × 39 mm). However, even these designations are incomplete descriptions of a particular type of ammunition and are thus often accompanied by additional information regarding the style of the cartridge base (e.g., rimmed, belted), the powder loading/pressure developed by the cartridge (+P, +P+), the type or weight of projectile used, etc.
Another common system of nomenclature lists the caliber of the projectile and the weapon for which it was originally developed or the manufacturer who originally developed it (e.g., .45 Automatic Colt Pistol [ACP]); 9 mm Luger/9 mm Parabellum (these are alternate names for the 9 × 19 mm). The suffix is therefore an important description, as completely different chamberings may utilize the same diameter bullet; although all use a bullet of approximately the same diameter, the .45 ACP cartridge is decidedly not the same as the .45 Long Colt (LC) or the .45 Glock Automatic Pistol (GAP), for example. If a cartridge proves popular, many manufacturers, in addition to the original manufacturer, may begin to produce weapons chambered for that cartridge.
More arcane systems of measurement are sometimes encountered in modern ammunition as holdovers from the transition from the black powder to the smokeless powder era. For example, the .45-70 Government indicates a cartridge developed in the early 1870s with a .45 caliber projectile, loaded with 70 grains of black powder. Although modern ".45-70 Govt" cartridges are now often loaded with smokeless powder to pressures beyond those sustainable by 19th-century firearms, the name of the cartridge stuck. Likewise, although developed at the dawn of the smokeless powder era, the .30-30 Winchester cartridge was named analogously to the old black powder convention. To further confuse the issue, although one of the major US military cartridges of the last century, the .30-06, also appears to conform to this nomenclature convention, it is actually so designated because of the year it was adopted (1906).
Finally, shotgun chamberings are typically described in terms of the gauge (e.g., 12-gauge, 16-gauge, 28-gauge). The gauge is determined by identifying the number of lead spheres with the same diameter as the bore that collectively weigh 1 lb. For example, a shotgun with a bore diameter of 0.729 inches is a 12-gauge shotgun, since 12 lead spheres with diameters of 0.729 weigh 1 lb. Thus, it will be clear that as the gauge increases, the diameter of the bore will decrease, and vice versa. The major exception to this nomenclature is the .410 shotgun chambering, which simply describes a weapon with a 0.410-inch bore.
Ammunition nomenclature is complex and frequently not intuitive, requiring in-depth historical knowledge for full understanding. Physicians who have completed years of biomedical training, run the gauntlet of impromptu quizzing during rounds, and passed board and licensure examinations will ruefully appreciate how such complexity can lead to apparent inconsistencies (e.g., that the .38 Special and .357 Magnum cartridges use the same caliber bullet; and that a .357 Magnum revolver can fire .38 Special cartridges, but not vice-versa) that are only truly resolved by highly specialized or historical knowledge of the subject matter. A rational resignation to a certain level of memorization of facts regarding the more commonly encountered cartridges, and access to more expert advice in the case of questions or when encountering more exotic ammunition, is entirely appropriate for the day-to-day practice of the forensic pathologist.

## Modern Small Arms

Modern small arms may be classified most generally into long guns and handguns, with the former group encompassing rifles and shotguns. Automatic weapons (machine guns and submachine guns) may also be included in this classification, but they are less commonly encountered in civilian practice. A possible additional category, falling astride handguns and long guns, may be defined as encompassing personal defense weapons.
Long guns are longer-barreled weapons with a stock designed to be braced against the shoulder of the shooter. Rifles are so named for their rifled barrels, which have spiraling parallel grooves cut into the bore. The raised portions of metal left between the grooves, called the lands, will impart spin to the projectile as it travels down the bore and cause it to be gyroscopically stabilized in flight. In conjunction with the ability to brace the weapon against the shoulder and having a longer sight radius, this tends to increase the practical accuracy of the weapon. In general, rifles can be chambered for more powerful ammunition than that found in handguns, although this is not always the case, as many rifles are chambered for intermediate rounds or rounds that were designed to be fired in handguns. Rifles are available in a variety of action types, including semiautomatic, bolt-action, lever-action, and single-shot, among others.
Shotguns are also longer-barreled weapons, but they have a smooth, non-rifled bore. Shotguns usually fire a charge consisting of multiple round shot pellets instead of a single projectile, although solid projectile loadings (slugs) are also used. Common action types include pump-action, semiautomatic, single-shot, and double-barreled varieties.
A handgun is designed to be held in the hands when fired and typically lacks any bracing mechanism. Handguns generally have short barrels relative to other types of arms, but these barrels are also typically rifled. Handguns may be further divided into revolvers (in which a revolving cylinder contains the cartridges to be fired) and pistols (which include semiautomatic or autoloading weapons firing from a spring-loaded magazine, typically using a reciprocating slide operated by the recoil impulse of the shot).

### Repeating firearm types

With relatively few (and mechanically fascinating) exceptions, muzzle-loading firearms historically were single-shot weapons. The advent of breech-loading firearms and the development of repeating firearms coincided, allowing multiple cartridges to be discharged rapidly from a magazine or cylinder before manual reloading was required. The following is a brief overview of the way cartridges are loaded into the chamber in various repeating firearm designs.
The earliest widely successful repeating firearm was the revolver. The revolver uses a cylinder with multiple chambers, into which cartridges are loaded, and traditionally uses a hammer strike to ignite the primer. For each successive shot, the cylinder rotates so a new chamber is indexed with the barrel. A feature of particular interest to the forensic pathologist is that revolvers from different manufacturers may rotate in opposite directions. This may result in a fatal outcome if an individual playing Russian roulette peeks to locate the cartridge, intending to dry-fire the weapon as long as the cartridge is not expected to rotate into firing position, but is unfamiliar with the direction of rotation of the cylinder.
Another relatively early repeating firearm design is the lever-action gun. Most typically, these weapons are loaded from a tubular magazine under the barrel, using a manually operated lever combined with the trigger guard of the weapon that simultaneously ejects a spent cartridge, loads a new round, and cocks a hammer.
Bolt-action weapons are another manually operated repeating firearm type, often loaded from an internal box magazine and usually striker-fired with a firing pin. After firing, the bolt is rotated using a handle, pulled to the rear to extract and eject the spent cartridge, and pushed forward to load the next round; during this process the spring-loaded firing pin is cocked.
Pump-action guns are likewise manually operated repeating firearms. These weapons are generally loaded by the reciprocating backward-and-forward action of a grip located on the "forearm" of the weapon, under the barrel. Retracting the pump backward opens the breech and extracts the spent cartridge; returning the pump forward loads a new cartridge. The firearm most classically associated with the pump-action is the shotgun, although pump-action rifles also exist.
Finally, semiautomatic (and automatic) weapons mechanically harness the recoil momentum or gas pressure of the discharging bullet/cartridge to complete the extraction/ejection of the spent casing and to load the fresh cartridge. Cartridges are usually fed into the firing mechanism via a spring-loaded magazine that holds multiple rounds. Such magazines are often rapidly interchangeable, facilitating quick reloading of the weapon. Because of this, multiple follow-up shots may be fired quickly without manual reloading or cocking of the firearm. When fired, recoil or gas pressure is typically used to drive a slide or bolt toward the rear of the weapon against a spring, which then returns the slide or bolt to the front. This reciprocating mechanism causes the fired case to be ejected from the firearm, a fresh cartridge to be loaded into the firing chamber, and the weapon to be cocked and readied for firing, often using a hammer. In a semiautomatic weapon, one pull of the trigger will discharge only a single round. This is in contrast to the fully automatic or selective fire weapons most commonly found in the hands of police or military agencies. Such firearms, represented by machine guns or submachine guns, can rapidly discharge multiple shots as long as the trigger is depressed.

### Firearm action mechanism types

Typical repeating firearm action types include single-action, double-action, and striker-fired mechanisms. In this context, the "action" generally describes how the firearm is designed to strike the primer and ignite the cartridge. A single-action firearm has a hammer that must be cocked for each pull of the trigger. This may be performed either by the shooter manually thumbing back the hammer, as in a single-action revolver (the Colt 1873 "Peacemaker" pattern revolver); by the shooter when retracting the slide or bolt of a single-action semiautomatic or automatic firearm (e.g., US M1911 and variants); or by the action of the recoiling slide or bolt after a shot is fired from such a weapon.
The characteristic feature of a double-action mechanism is that the pull of the trigger first cocks and then drops the hammer to discharge the weapon. In addition, some double-action mechanisms with an exposed hammer will also allow the operator to manually cock the hammer of the firearm before a pull of the trigger, meaning that the shooter can choose to fire the weapon in either single or double action. This action type is well represented by more modern revolver designs that once comprised the majority of the law enforcement handgun market. Relatively more recently, this market was dominated by semiautomatic handguns, which are most properly described as having a double-action/single-action (DA/SA) operation. In these semiautomatic handguns, the hammer can initially be de-cocked, requiring the long, double-action trigger pull to cock and release the hammer for the first shot. The action of the slide following the first shot cocks the hammer, and the second and subsequent shots are fired in single-action mode.
The DA/SA mechanism described above results in the need for the shooter to master two different types of trigger pulls: The long, heavy, double-action trigger pull for the first shot, and the shorter, lighter, single-action trigger pull for follow-up shots. Double-action only (DAO) and striker-fired mechanisms attempt, in part, to create weapons with single, reproducible trigger pulls of intermediate length and weight. The DAO weapon uses a hammer that operates only in double-action mode; the striker-fired pistol utilizes a spring-loaded firing pin rather than a hammer to ignite the primer. Striker-fired mechanisms are also common in bolt-action rifles.
The pathologist should be aware that newer firearm designs, proliferating in response to popular trends and legislative strictures, can somewhat blur the above lines. For example, a weapon matching the legal definition of a pistol may be chambered to fire a cartridge historically associated with a rifle; a weapon legally defined as a rifle may be chambered in a pistol caliber; a smoothbore weapon with a pistol grip designed to fire shotshells may be legally defined as neither a pistol, a long gun, or a shotgun. When in doubt, the pathologist should request specific details regarding the brand of the weapon and the ammunition for which it is chambered. A more detailed discussion of firearms, ammunition, and their functions is beyond the scope of this article, but, again, excellent discussions may be found in the Additional Resources section.

## Ballistics

Although an in-depth knowledge of projectile ballistics is both beyond the scope of this article and unnecessary for the day-to-day practice of forensic pathology, a basic understanding will elucidate many features of firearm wounds and may assist practitioners to define their precise field of expertise when questioned by outside stakeholders.
In general, the study of ballistics involves three main subdivisions: Interior, exterior, and terminal.

### Interior ballistics

Interior ballistics involves the study of the actions of the projectile from the instant the primer is struck and ignites the propellant charge until the projectile exits the muzzle of the gun. Thus, in this context, interior refers to the forces acting upon and the behavior of the projectile inside the firearm.
In brief, as the propellant burns, a high-pressure and high-temperature environment is created within the breech and bore of the weapon, which drives the projectile down the barrel. As this occurs, the bearing surfaces of the bullet fired from a rifled handgun or long gun will engage the rifling of the barrel, and spin will be imparted to the projectile. As already mentioned, and as discussed further below, the aspects of forensic significance to interior ballistics include the velocity generated, the amount of propellant burned, and the amount of residues (combustion products, burning and unburnt gunpowder, jet of heated gas) that exit the muzzle with the projectile and may create findings on skin or clothing. Recall that the kinetic energy of a projectile depends both on the mass of the projectile as well as the square of its velocity; thus, the understanding that rifles generally are capable of generating higher projectile velocities than handguns will serve to explain the empirical observation of the typically more devastating nature of rifle wounds when compared to those from handguns.
It is during the period defined by interior ballistics that toolmarks may be produced by the weapon on the bullet or cartridge case. Although the evaluation of these toolmarks is not generally the duty of the physician, awareness of their existence and importance will facilitate the proper handling of evidence recovered from the body.

### Exterior ballistics

Exterior ballistics studies the behavior of the projectile in flight, from the instant it exits the muzzle of the weapon to the point at which it strikes the target. A point of particular importance to the understanding of projectile behavior is that, as a bullet exits the muzzle of a firearm, it may yaw at an angle which is usually relatively small in absolute terms (a matter of a few degrees). However, when discussing the flight of a (typically) oblong projectile at very high speed, even a relatively low-angle yaw can produce a significant force due to air resistance, which acts to cause the bullet to tumble. The above-described rifling of many modern firearm barrels (shotguns excepted) is designed specifically to counter this "overturning moment" and prevent the bullet from tumbling, by gyroscopically stabilizing the bullet in flight using its angular momentum from the spin produced by the rifling. As an intuitive aid to the understanding of this phenomenon, readers can consider the action of a child's spinning top or the motion of an American football when well-thrown with a spiral.

### Terminal ballistics and wound ballistics

Finally, the termination of the flight of the bullet as it impacts a target is the focus of terminal ballistics ; when that target is a living organism, this study is synonymous with wound ballistics . This represents the area of expertise of the forensic pathologist, for whom the evaluation of victims of gunshot wounds represents a significant aspect of practice.
The spinning projectile in flight through the air, as described above, is gyroscopically stable due to its angular momentum. However, as the projectile transitions from the medium of the atmosphere to the (orders of magnitude denser) medium of the body, the rotation of the bullet is no longer sufficient to stabilize it against the overturning forces generated. As a result, the projectile will tend to tumble (except in the case of rapid deformation to create a situation of "shoulder stabilization," seen with hollow-point projectiles designed to "mushroom" upon impact with the target).
Thus, the shape, construction, and behavior of the projectile can significantly affect the nature of the wound(s) produced. Tumbling and/or deformation will tend both to increase the surface area and decrease the sectional density of the projectile presented to the tissue; these changes will increase the amount of energy transferred from the projectile into the tissue, causing the projectile to decelerate rapidly.
The typical handgun bullet is relatively short and travels at a relatively low velocity. As one shifts their inspection of a handgun bullet from the front to the side, the surface area and the sectional density of the bullet are relatively unchanged, and the amount of kinetic energy possessed by the bullet is limited by the lower velocity. Thus, the tumbling of a handgun bullet does not generally factor significantly in the wound produced, and handgun rounds designed for personal defense have traditionally been designed to "mushroom," undergoing a flattening deformation of the nose upon impact, which increases the surface area of the projectile.
In contrast, the typically higher velocity (and hence higher energy) rifle bullet tends toward a more elongated, sleek profile; when viewed from the front, the surface area presented by the tip of the bullet is low while the sectional density is high. As the rifle bullet is viewed from the side, the sectional density drops significantly while the surface area increases correspondingly. Because the tumbling of the bullet does not occur instantaneously, the wound track of such a rifle bullet (as preserved and described in ballistics gelatin) will initially be rather narrow; as the projectile yaws, more and more energy will be lost to the tissue as the surface area presented to the tissue increases and the sectional density decreases; the wound track will subsequently expand significantly.
To recapitulate, the kinetic energy possessed by the projectile depends on its mass and velocity as discussed above; the energy lost by a projectile to the tissue depends significantly upon the surface area and sectional density presented to the medium of the tissue as the bullet passes through it. As the projectile deforms or tumbles, it will tend to present a higher surface area and lower sectional density to the tissue and will therefore deposit more energy into the tissue. This also generates correspondingly higher forces on the projectile, and in the case of particularly high-speed or lightly constructed projectiles, classically from centerfire rifle cartridges or fragmenting/frangible bullets, these forces may overcome the projectile's integrity and cause it to fragment. Fragmentation will produce the radiographic finding of the "lead snowstorm," and by multiplying the amount of surface area presented to the tissue while progressively decreasing sectional density, fragmentation will tend to magnify the energy transferred into the tissues.
A particular point of interest involving the same concepts is the behavior of a destabilized projectile or projectile fragments, which may strike the body if the projectile has first struck an intervening or intermediary target. The unpredictable nature of the destabilized projectile or fragments explains the wide variety of atypical entrance wounds that may be encountered when an individual is shot through an intervening target, such as a window or vehicle door.
Another recreational or sporting analogy may be helpful. The importance of sectional density and surface area to terminal ballistics will be readily understood by anyone who has experienced the difference between a perfect dive into a pool of water (high angle entrance, with the small surface area of the palms presented to the water surface and the high sectional density of the rest of the body behind them; deep submersion into the water) and a back- or belly-flop (high surface area, low sectional density; energy rapidly expended near the surface, with only shallow passage into the water).

## Epidemiology

The United States is a nation with a long history and tradition of private firearm ownership rights. There were an estimated 393 million privately owned firearms in the United States in 2018, and the number generally rises each year.
The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reported a total of 48,830 firearm deaths in 2021; included in this total were 20,958 firearm homicides and 26,328 firearm suicides. These numbers represented an increase from 39,740 total firearm deaths in 2018.
The reasons for these injuries, mitigation strategies, and the role of firearms regulation as a preventative measure have engendered intense and ongoing public debate, particularly in recent decades. Political beliefs notwithstanding, it is clear that all forensic pathologists practicing in the United States will encounter the need to interpret firearm injuries on a regular basis.

## Aspects of Firearm Wounds

Forensic pathologists seek to accomplish four main goals during the examination and characterization of gunshot wounds. These goals are to establish the following:
- Trajectory of the missile or projectile (implicit in this goal is the necessity of identifying entrance and exit defects, if the wound is perforating [passing entirely through the body])
- Range of fire
- Type and extent of the injuries inflicted by the projectile(s)
- Type of weapon used to inflict the injury (implicit in this goal is the necessity of recovering any retained projectile, if the wound is penetrating [entering but not exiting the body])

### Trajectory of the missile or projectile

The trajectory, or direction, of a projectile through the body may be approximated, but it must be kept in mind that this does not necessarily equate to the trajectory of the bullet through space. Because the bodies of the victim and shooter are not static and may move about in three dimensions during a shooting event, the standard anatomic position is utilized to describe the trajectory of a bullet through a body. By convention, this assumes that the victim is standing upright, face and feet forward, with arms stretched downward along the side of the torso, and with palms forward. All directions are given based on this positioning of the body, which is very likely not the actual position the victim was in when shot.
However, occasionally the pathway of a bullet through the body may give some indication of the actual position of the body when shot. For example, a bullet may exit one part of the body (such as an arm) only to re-enter the torso or other body part. This may be suggested by the characteristics of the wounds, as well as the juxtaposition of the two wounds on certain positions of the body parts, which can be demonstrated at the time of autopsy examination.

### Range of fire

Range of fire, or muzzle-to-target distance, may be divided into contact, near contact, intermediate, and distant categories, with various subtypes also demonstrable. Contact and intermediate-range wounds are often collectively referred to as close-range wounds.
Determination of the range of fire is based on the characteristics of a firearm wound, features of the wound that have been imparted by material issuing from the muzzle of the firearm other than the bullet, or from features due to direct interactions between the target and the firearm itself. Material from the firearm muzzle may take the form of soot, hot gas, gunpowder particles, or other material, and the effects of this material are discussed in more detail below. Because the very presence of material issuing from the firearm may be influenced by the physical environment of the shooting — most obviously in the case of such material being blocked from the body by an intervening target — the prudent forensic pathologist may opt to characterize wounds lacking evidence of close-range firing as being of indeterminate rather than distant range.
The range of fire has obvious relevance to issues such as whether a wound is self-inflicted or inflicted by another person, the truth of proffered explanations of shooting events, and the validity of self-defense arguments.

### Type and extent of the injuries inflicted by the projectile(s)

The types of injuries caused by firearm wounds have significance above and beyond the cause and manner of death determination. The assessment of damage to vital organs, central nervous system components, or bony framework may allow a forensic pathologist to address many issues of legal significance, such as survival time or the time required for a wound to incapacitate a victim. It may also be up to the forensic pathologist to dispel much of the erroneous and mythological concepts that abound regarding firearm wounds, such as "stopping power," instantaneous death, or bodies being hurled about by bullet impacts.
As previously noted, the study of the interaction of a missile, projectile, or bullet with a target is referred to as terminal ballistics ; wound ballistics is a subset of terminal ballistics wherein the target is a human body. It is this field that is of great importance to clinicians, forensic pathologists, those in law enforcement and military agencies, and others. Much work in this area has been done to attempt to elucidate the effects of a bullet on a victim's body; many of these efforts have centered on explanation of the two primary traumatic effects of a projectile; that is, formation of the permanent and temporary cavities .
When a bullet perforates tissue, it forms a permanent cavity or track that persists and results from the crushing and laceration of body tissues. Because of the high velocity of the bullet, it will also form a temporary cavity caused by the sonic shock wave that radiates from the passing projectile, causing the tissue to be thrown radially away from the projectile. The temporary cavity, thus formed, will rapidly (over the course of milliseconds) undulate or pulsate in a progressively smaller fashion before collapsing to the final permanent cavity. The size and shape of this cavity will depend on a number of factors, primarily the velocity of the projectile.
As noted, kinetic energy increases by the square of bullet velocity. Hence, a high-velocity projectile will tend to cause a larger temporary cavity than a lower velocity one. Because of this, temporary cavities are usually not significant in handgun injuries, but they are of much greater significance in high-velocity rifle wounds. To reiterate, in addition to velocity, the behavior of a projectile in tissue (e.g., stabilized, destabilized, fragmented; surface area presented; sectional density) will also bear on the size and shape of the cavity. Depending on the elasticity and other characteristics of the tissue in question, the formation of the temporary cavity will have varying effects on the body.

### Type of weapon

The general type of weapon may sometimes be suggested by the characteristics of a wound, and the general type or individual weapon may be identified by the recovery of the projectile causing a wound, with appropriate criminalistic analysis. In addition to the determination of bullet caliber (diameter) and type, telltale markings may be present on recovered projectiles or casings, allowing matching of the projectile or case to a firearm. Such markings are created when a bullet or case contacts the barrel rifling, breech face, chamber, ejector, or other parts of a firearm, and they may be sufficiently unique to qualify as "individual characteristics" that allow matching of a recovered bullet or fragment to the specific weapon that fired it. In other cases, only general characteristics such as the likely type or brand of the firearm may be determined, but these features are still useful. The importance and necessity of recovering retained projectiles should be understood, considering the potential wealth of information that may be gleaned from their examination.
Although there are well-systematized principles that allow for categorization of various types of firearm wounds, all such injuries are, in fact, unique, and exceptional variability may be seen in any large series of wounds. Some injuries defy categorization, and in any such case, the forensic pathologist must honestly assess the wound and the amount of interpretive information that may be extracted from it, without attempts to rigidly "pigeonhole" the wound for the sake of classification. A rigorous assessment of all features of the injury, thorough scene investigation, examination of clothing items of the victim, and examination and understanding of the implicated firearm (if available) provide the best opportunity for correct evaluation of the injury.

## Forensic Investigation of Firearm Deaths

As deaths due to firearm injuries will fall into categories of unnatural manner of death, all should come under the jurisdiction of a medical examiner, coroner, or justice of the peace, depending on the death investigation system in the local area. In addition to local statutes, published professional standards require forensic pathologists in such settings to investigate deaths due to violence, which clearly includes firearm wounds. Although some firearm injuries produce relatively rapid incapacitation or death, it is not uncommon for a person to initially survive a firearm wound but then eventually succumb to remote complications of the wound days, weeks, months, or even years later. However, such a case remains of medicolegal interest if the death can be shown to be directly or indirectly related to the initial wound.
Although some authors might question the need for a detailed medicolegal investigation into deaths manifestly due to firearm wounds (after all, "We know why they died, don't we?"), the proper examination of such a case may clarify the cause of death, the identity of the assailant, and the manner of death. These are often areas of contention. Was the firearm wound death a homicide, accident, or suicide? Was the shot fired in self-defense? Was the victim attacking or fleeing from the shooter? Was the fatal bullet fired from the accused person's gun? Proper interpretation of trajectory, range of fire, and other findings may help to answer these and many more questions. Recovery of a bullet may serve to exonerate or implicate a particular firearm in the incident.
Because of the seriousness and gravity of the legal questions to be answered, these examinations should be conducted by a physician properly trained and certified in forensic pathology.

## Forensic Scene Examination

The scene examination in the case of a firearm wound victim may provide invaluable information. At the scene, care should be taken not to disturb blood patterns on the floor, as they may give indications as to the site of injury and the degree of activity of the decedent after injury. The position and location of the firearm should be carefully noted, if present, and its position of battery (e.g., is the firearm loaded, is a round chambered, are fired rounds in the cylinder, and is the weapon cocked?) is of primary importance.
A person familiar with the type of weapon in question should recover the firearm and clear it if necessary, taking note of the position of fired casings relative to the hammer of a revolver, the presence or absence of a magazine in a semiautomatic handgun, the slide position or any jams noted in a semiautomatic handgun, the position of a pump shotgun slide, and the presence and condition of any safety mechanisms, among other variables. All of this information may have a significant bearing on the manner of death determination at a later date.
The type of ammunition present at the scene, either in a firearm or elsewhere, should be described in detail (brand, caliber, bullet style) so that any recovered bullet can be immediately compared with what was found in the firearm at the scene.
Any projectile found beneath the decedent, stuck to clothing, or nearby at the scene should be photographed and recovered at that time to prevent the possibility of loss during transport. If firearm primer residue sampling is desired, it may be collected at the scene, or the hands may be covered by paper bags (not plastic, as condensation may form when the body is refrigerated) before transport to prevent evidentiary loss during transport.
The presence of bullet strikes on the walls of a room, a vehicle, or other nearby objects should be noted, as these can serve as reference points for later reconstruction of the position of a shooter when combined with a trajectory through the body.
The proliferation of potential sources of video footage represents an information source of surpassing value, which is far more widely available to present-day pathologists than to any of their predecessors. Searching the scene for the presence of any surveillance cameras that might have captured the shooting, the events immediately preceding, or the aftermath, as well as identifying any available footage from body-worn cameras, spectator-captured video, or video from other sources such as dashboard cameras, should therefore represent a high priority of the scene examination and death investigation. Review of such video footage is highly recommended even when the cause and/or manner of death are readily apparent and the gunshot wounds easily characterized, as such footage can help address ancillary questions about the case. When difficulties regarding the cause or manner of death present themselves, or when wounds are morphologically challenging to characterize, video footage and scene examination can provide even greater assistance.

## Trace Evidence

In evaluating firearm wounds, the decedent's clothing should be examined, if at all possible, to attempt to match the defects on it with those on the body, as well as for the presence or absence of residues from the firearm discharge. These residues will predominately take the form of soot or gunpowder particles.
Soot usually appears as a dark gray, featureless deposit surrounding the bullet defect on clothing. It is readily visible on light-colored fabric, but it may be difficult or impossible to discern with the naked eye on dark fabrics. Soot may also be obscured by blood, dirt, or other substances. Blood, in particular, may darken as it dries and should not be confused with soot deposition.
Gunpowder particles, contrary to common misconception, are often not dark in color. They frequently have a light color, often with a yellow or greenish hue, and may be seen some distance from the bullet perforation, depending on the muzzle-to-target distance. These particles will have varying shapes, depending on the type of powder in use. Common configurations include ball (or spherical), flattened ball, flake, and cylindrical powders.
Gunpowder grains may be difficult to see with the naked eye; viewing under magnification may assist in identifying and/or confirming the presence of particles. A loupe lens or a handheld dissecting microscope head can be useful in examining such areas. In a case of obvious or possible foul play, garments should be carefully air dried, placed in individual paper bags, and either submitted for further criminalistic examination or retained for potential future examination as required.
If a bullet has passed through an intermediary target before striking the decedent, fragments of that target may be adherent to clothing or the surface of the body. Small fragments of lead or copper jacket material may also be found in a similar circumstance, due to break-up of the bullet. A "plug" of fabric or other such material may be found within the cavity of a hollow-point bullet that has passed through clothing, a pillow, a car seat, or other objects.
Examination of the decedent for other "trace" evidence, in addition to that associated with firearm wound assessment, may be performed as appropriate. This might include fingernail clippings, sexual assault swabs, hair combings, or the collection of any other needed materials.

## Primer Residues

In addition to gunpowder and combustion residues deposited on the clothing or skin due to the discharge of a firearm, gunshot primer residues may also be deposited. These are generated by the discharge of the cartridge primer, which vaporizes the metallic compounds within that primer and causes them to be discharged as a cloud around the firearm. These compounds are most commonly barium, lead, and antimony, although not all are present in every brand or type of primer.
The primer residues may be collected by swabbing or using tape to lift them from the skin surfaces and then testing for the residues by various laboratory means, usually SEM-EDX or inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. The testing of primer residues is more properly considered in a dissertation related to criminalistics, but the interpretation of the residues is an area often posed to the forensic pathologist.
Traditionally, it was believed that the distribution of residues on the decedent's hands could help to discern whether a gunshot wound was self-inflicted or not. The premise was that in a self-inflicted wound, residues would be deposited on the back of the firing hand, because the palm that was grasping the firearm would be "protected" or shielded by its position, whereas in an assault situation, a victim could throw up the hand(s) instinctively as a shield, allowing primer residues to be deposited on the palms.
The problem with this view is that the cloud of gunshot primer residue is widely distributed in the area of a discharging firearm and may settle on any convenient surface, even those several feet from the firearm. This would include any surfaces of the body of the decedent, as well as those of the assailant. Additionally, primer residues can be transferred from one surface to another. Unsurprisingly, firearms themselves are often contaminated with primer residues, so merely picking up a recently fired gun could deposit residues on the palm.
In practice, the presence or absence of primer residues on the hands has limited utility in the determination of the manner of death in postmortem cases.

## Imaging Studies

### Radiography

The clothed body of a firearm wound victim should always be thoroughly radiographed. All parts of the body affected by a firearm wound must be imaged, and it may be that even sites distant from the entrance wound need to be examined. For example, a firearm wound of the shoulder can pass through the arm, chest, and abdomen, terminating in the decedent's leg under certain circumstances.
Some modern full-body X-ray scanners can provide high-resolution full-body radiographs in a matter of seconds or minutes, making this process much more efficient and complete.
If appropriate bullets or fragments are not found in initial radiographs, repeat images of other body areas should be obtained, taking care to cover all areas of soft tissue and clothing. This is particularly important in obese decedents, in whom more than one X-ray plate may be needed to fully image a level of the torso. Such individuals may require a longer exposure or higher kilovoltage of the X-ray beam to obtain adequate visualization. Lateral views of retained projectiles will be of tremendous assistance in the localization of the bullet in many cases.
In some cases, projectiles outside the body may be present (whether underlying the body or entangled in clothing or hair). In addition, some types of clothing may incorporate extensive amounts of radiopaque material (metallic decorations, buttons, snaps, etc.), and these as well as personal property within the clothing or on/in the body (e.g., coins, keys, jewelry) may complicate interpretation of the radiographs of the clothed body. Additional radiographs obtained after all clothing and property have been removed can clarify which projectiles truly remain lodged in the body.
When discussing firearm radiography, it is important to remember that magnification effects may make projectiles and fragments appear to be much larger than their actual size (see the following image). For this reason, one should never attempt to estimate the caliber of a projectile from a radiograph without appropriate consideration of the focal length of the X-ray tube and the distance from the imaged projectile to the X-ray plate.
The pathologist should also be aware that some exotic ammunition may have bullet components that are less visible on radiographs than typical lead or copper bullets and jackets. These may include aluminum bullet jackets, plastic sabots or caps, or other components.
Some forensic pathologists have found fluoroscopy to be useful in localizing projectiles within the body, although this technique is far less common than standard radiography.

### Postmortem computed tomography scanning

CT scanning of decedents remains expensive, but PMCT has emerged as a valuable tool for examinations of decedents and is becoming increasingly common in forensic pathology, particularly in research. Several studies have concluded that PMCT cannot replace the traditional autopsy, but both evaluations contribute to a complete picture of the cause and manner of death.
In a study by Kirchhoff et al, PMCT data in 51 gunshot-related deaths were evaluated by two reader groups and compared to the gold standard autopsy for the determination of number of gunshots, localization of gunshot injury, caliber, and direction of the gunshot differentiating between entry and exit wound, as well as associated injury to surrounding tissue. Based upon the results, the investigators concluded that physical autopsy is better than PMCT in terms of localizing most gunshot injuries. However, they also concluded that PMCT is better than physical autopsy in locating fragmented bullets/fragment clouds.
Giraudo et al found evidence that micro-CT analysis may be a useful screening tool for differentiating entry gunshot wounds from exit gunshot wounds when the covering textiles are contaminated, damaged, or missing. Firing trials were performed in a ballistic laboratory on 60 sections of human calves enveloped by a layer of fabric (cotton, jeans, leather, or nylon) and 15 controls consisting of bare calves. Micro-CT analysis showed the presence of radiopaque material on all entrance wounds (with a statistically significant difference between cases and controls), but no GSR-like deposits were detected in exit wounds.

## Gross Examination and Findings

All wounds, as well as the hands, should be carefully examined before cleaning the body. Soot and other residues on the body may be washed away with cleaning, and they may only be seen on initial inspection of the victim. Photographs of a wound before and after cleaning may be valuable for the documentation of such findings.

### External examination: Localization and measurement of wound(s)

Wound(s) should be localized on the body. Measurements to locate a wound may be taken from the top of the head and right or left of the midline of the body, but other reference points may be used as well. Use of consistent reference points will allow reconstruction of wound trajectories, which is the primary reason for taking such measurements.
In addition to giving the precise measured location of a wound, its location may also be generally described using easily identifiable anatomic landmarks. For example, a firearm wound of the chest might be described as "36 cm from the top of the head, 4 cm left of midline, and above and medial to the left nipple."
Next, the wound itself should be measured, generally in two dimensions (e.g., 0.5 × 0.7 cm) and described by its shape (e.g., round, oval, slit-like, stellate, ragged). The presence or absence of any abrasion rim should be noted, with its width and extent measured. Any soot or powder stippling should be noted and its extent measured as well.

### External wounds vs number of internal bullets/projectiles

Before commencing the autopsy of a firearm injury victim, it is important to compare the wounds noted externally with the number of bullets or projectiles found in the body on radiographic examination. Because a bullet may either exit the body, producing both entrance and exit wounds (perforating), or remain in the body, producing only an entrance wound (penetrating), the number of wounds plus the number of bullets found on radiographs should generally be an even number. If there is a discrepancy, consideration should be given to the possibility of missed wounds (check the mouth, perineum, axillae, gluteal cleft, and other "hidden" spots), missed projectiles (increase the extent of the radiological examination), projectiles having exited the body but remaining tangled within clothing or hair and thus visualized on radiography, or fragmentation of bullets within the body (a single entrance wound resulting in two "projectiles" seen radiographically). Graze or tangential wounds (where the projectile did not enter the body but simply passed along its surface), as well as shallow defects created by the impact of a bullet or fragment which rebounded from the skin without creating a deeper wound track can also result in an odd number of defects in a given case.
It is possible for a small-caliber bullet to enter the heart or a large blood vessel and be subsequently embolized or pumped "downstream" for some distance by cardiovascular action, removing it from the portion of the body that is initially chosen for radiography. If surgery has been performed, consider that a bullet may have been purposely or inadvertently removed during the procedure (requesting any surgically excised specimens for radiography and examination might be indicated). Small-caliber projectiles may be picked up by accident with a sponge, dropped, or suctioned away. It is also possible that multiple "entrance wounds" may have been produced by fragmentation of a projectile or an intermediary target, or that "exit wounds" may have been produced by the tearing of the skin by the superficial passage of a bullet that did not exit, or by the exiting of bone fragments instead of a bullet.

### Internal examination: Evaluation and special dissections

It is important to attempt to develop a preliminary idea of wound pathways and entrance vs exit wounds before commencing the internal examination of the body. This is sometimes complicated as discussed above, is subject to change as the examination commences, and may not be feasible in all cases. Removal of organs should be delayed until all wound tracks have been identified, if possible, because evisceration irreversibly alters many anatomic relationships, rendering further analysis of tracks difficult. Of course, some wound tracks (e.g., through the retroperitoneum or posterior mediastinum) cannot be fully evaluated without removing some of the overlying organs and structures.
Blood accumulations within the major cavities of the body should be measured as an adjunct to assessing the mechanism of death, time of survival, and likelihood of survival, among other determinations. Other findings, such as major cardiac or vascular injuries, blood aspiration, or cardiac air embolus, may assist in addressing these questions at a later date.
Ideally, the internal examination of the victim of a gunshot wound would be a simple matter of following a given projectile through the body and identifying perforations or penetrations of the affected organs/structures. Forensic pathologists, however, practice not in an ideal world but in the real world, and this deceptively simplistic task is often highly complex. Although a single firearm wound may be easily tracked through the body, the presence of multiple wounds may present problems in analysis, particularly if the decedent was shot with a small-caliber weapon, if extensively destructive wound tracks are in close apposition or when wound tracks intersect, or if intermediary targets caused the projectiles to fragment. Should the victim have survived long enough to be hospitalized and undergo surgical intervention, pathologists will find themselves instead following the surgeon through the body, identifying alterations attributable to postsurgical changes rather than directly visualizing the wound track(s), and therefore relying significantly upon medical documentation to provide information about the unaltered wound(s). In some cases, a given individual wound track cannot be traced to an acceptable degree of certainty. In such cases, it is important for the pathologist to honestly state so in the autopsy report.
If a projectile enters the spine, it is important to open the spinal canal and examine the spinal cord to determine the presence or absence of cord damage, as activity of the shooting victim after injury is often a matter of question in court. For the same reason, damage to the brain, supporting axial skeleton, and extremities should be explored and documented with the intent of addressing potential questions about consciousness, locomotion, or ability to fight.

### Autopsy documentation

In preparing an autopsy report in cases of firearm wounds, it is preferable to describe each separate firearm wound in its entirety, rather than describing all entrance wounds, then all exit wounds, and then internal injuries. For example, a firearm wound would best be documented by describing the location, size, and character of the entrance wound, then the path of the wound through the body, including the injured structures, and, finally, the recovered projectile or exit wound (size, location, description).
Associated findings, such as hemothoraces, may then be mentioned, followed by an approximate direction of the wound track, using the standard anatomic position as a reference point. For example, a wound path may be described as "front to back, left to right, and slightly downward." The assignment of a numeric angle measurement to the wound track should be discouraged, as this presents a false level of precision to the reader of the report — and, potentially, to a jury.
If, as discussed above, the individual wound track cannot be definitively traced, the autopsy report should reflect the points of uncertainty, describe the regions of the body affected by confluent wound tracks, or document surgical alterations as applicable.
Whether documented in a separate section or associated with the relevant individual gunshot wound description, any findings on the clothing related to the gunshot wounds represent an important part of the autopsy report.
The overriding concern in such a report is that the description must paint a picture of the wound as observed by the forensic pathologist so that it may be clearly understood by the reader.

## Photographic Documentation

Photographic documentation is of critical importance in the examination of firearm wounds, as it not only allows for secondary review of the autopsy by consultants or opposing experts but also allows the autopsy physician a chance for further review and/or consideration after completion of the examination. The National Association of Medical Examiners Forensic Autopsy Standards and Accreditation Checklist criteria require that photographs be taken in the case of significant injuries. Photographs should be of high quality, such that an observer can readily distinguish salient characteristics of the wound from the images.
In general, photographs of wounds before cleaning are, at best, difficult to interpret; nonetheless, such photographs can provide valuable information and documentation of the appearance of the body and wound(s) when first viewed, without any alteration. Thus, overall photographs of a victim before undressing and cleaning their body can provide useful information about things such as blood droplet patterns, position of clothing, and other details. If soot or gunpowder particles are noted around a wound, they should be photographed before any cleaning, as soot and loosely adherent gunpowder particles may be removed in the cleaning process. Should any later objections arise that cleaning the body and removing the clothing have somehow altered the appearance of the wound, such objections can be refuted.
For the definitive photographic documentation of wounds, the body should be cleaned, wiped dry, and all extraneous items (towels, scalpels, or other instruments) removed from the field before photography. Overall photographs showing the location of the wound(s) on the body should be taken, followed by close-up photographs of each wound. If scales are utilized, they should be in the same plane as the wound itself and should be placed unobtrusively at the edge of the photograph. Similarly, if an identifying case number is in the photograph, it too should be at the edge of the photograph in an unobtrusive manner.
If wounds are in a hair-bearing area such that they are obscured by the overlying hair, the area should next be carefully shaved to allow adequate examination of the wound and further photodocumentation. Examination and photography should take place immediately after shaving, because tiny nicks caused by shaving may mimic stippling once drying takes place.
It can be difficult to discern information from photographs of internal findings owing to bloody fields and the difficulty of reducing complex three-dimensional (3D) pathways to two-dimensional (2D) photographs. If photographs of internal findings are used, great care must be taken to clean the field and remove as much residual blood and other matter as possible to avoid distractions in the photograph and clarify the subject intended to be documented. Pointers and clearly definable anatomic points of reference may be included in the photographs to assist in interpretation. Ultimately, however, the most useful information provided by an internal photograph may simply be the documentation that a given structure or organ has been struck by "a" projectile. In all but the simplest of cases (e.g., single gunshot wounds), the characterization and tracing of wound tracks through the body solely by review of the photographs alone is effectively impossible and should not be expected of the pathologist by outside stakeholders.

## Special Handling

It is of critical importance to properly handle recovered evidence in firearm wound cases.
Clothing items should be spread out or hung up and allowed to air dry before being individually placed in paper bags for retention or crime laboratory examination.
The handling of bullets or other evidence will vary somewhat according to locales, and pathologists should consult with their local law enforcement agencies or crime laboratories to determine specific procedures for evidence handling. However, in general, bullets or fragments removed from a decedent should be gently rinsed (in plain water; do not use soaps or bleach) to remove blood and excess tissue. If the projectile is deformed and tissue is entangled in or trapped on the projectile, gentle rubbing with a toothbrush or similar tool may be useful. Following this cleansing, the projectiles should then be dried, wrapped in clean absorbent paper, and placed in an evidence envelope with appropriate labeling of the source of the bullet, case number, and other information. The envelope should be sealed with evidence tape and signed by the pathologist, then transported to the appropriate law enforcement agency or crime laboratory using a documented chain of custody.
In the past, it was customary for pathologists removing bullets to engrave their initials or some type of code onto the base of a removed bullet before releasing it. In theory, this would allow the pathologist to later definitively identify the bullet at trial. In fact, however, this procedure does not guarantee that the bullet is properly identified, and in practice, such engraving is exceedingly difficult to perform on a small projectile or to visualize when seated in a dimly lit courtroom. The best guarantee of the authenticity of the firearms evidence is a well-documented chain of custody. Some pathologists have found it helpful to photograph each removed projectile next to the labeled envelope into which it is to be placed, but even this is no assurance of authenticity in and of itself.
Shot cups or wadding from a shotgun wound should be recovered, rinsed, dried, and then handled in a manner similar to that noted above. Felt wads may be saturated with blood and body fluids and thus should be allowed to dry before packaging. When dealing with shotgun wounds due to birdshot, there may be innumerable pellets in the body, and recovery of all the pellets is impractical. In such cases, a sampling of representative pellets should be recovered (a dozen or so would usually suffice). In examining wounds due to buckshot, all pellets should be recovered, as the larger buckshot pellets are of comparable size to common rifle and handgun rounds. The forensic pathologist can imagine how the unexpected recovery of, for instance, a jacketed handgun projectile among lead buckshot pellets could significantly alter the character of such a case.
In the case of rifle wounds or handgun wounds with extensive fragmentation of the projectile(s), large fragments should be recovered, and the radiographs examined closely to identify any substantial jacketing fragments, which represent the highest yield from an evidentiary perspective. However, it may not be possible or practical to recover all tiny fragments from a wound, nor are minute metallic fragments of any compelling forensic interest in most cases. It should be remembered that magnification effects may cause a very tiny fragment to seem deceptively large on radiographs.
It is important to avoid contacting bullets or large fragments with metallic objects such as probes, forceps, or scalpel blades. Because the potential exists to examine the bearing surfaces of a bullet for comparison purposes, scratches or other marks inadvertently placed on such a surface could potentially interfere with criminalistic examination. The adequately stocked forensic morgue should include forceps specialized for the recovery of metallic projectiles (e.g., those entirely composed of plastic or with the tips covered by rubber).

## Microscopic Examination and Findings

Although the microscopic appearance of firearm wounds has been studied and described, the histology of firearm wounds rarely provides much meaningful information to the forensic pathologist. Wounds will show crush of tissue with hemorrhage, and if the interval between injury and death is sufficiently long, typical healing changes will be evident. Soot and powder grains may be seen, particularly in the case of contact wounds, which will also show superficial thermal alteration of the tissues (see the image below).
The histological presence of soot or powder grains within a wound track should not be used to infer information regarding the range of fire, as soot or powder may be carried into a wound by a projectile fired from a long distance. In fact, soot can sometimes be seen histologically in sections from a firearm exit wound. Range of fire determinations should be made on the basis of gross examination.
The use of bullet cytology has been reported in the literature. In such cases, bullets are rinsed in an appropriate buffer solution, and cytological preparations are prepared from the fluid. Although potentially useful in some situations, this procedure is not commonly utilized in practice. It is also possible to identify nonorganic material from intermediary targets on the surface of a bullet that has passed through that material. Electron microscopy with elemental analysis has been utilized for such purposes or for characterizing tiny metallic fragments or metallic deposits on bone.

## General Principles of Wound Interpretation

Various types of firearms may produce wounds that have different characteristics reflective of the type of weapon inflicting the injury. However, many characteristics of firearm wounds are also similar across the range of weapons. We first explore these basic characteristics, which are, in general, associated with handgun wounds. For a much more detailed treatment of this topic, the reader is referred to the classic textbook on the subject by DiMaio.

### Entrance wounds

Firearm entrance wounds are typically round to oval, with smooth edges and a zone of epidermal loss surrounding the wound edge (see the image below). This zone of epidermal damage was traditionally described (and continues to be described) as a marginal "abrasion." It was believed to be caused by the rubbing or scraping of surrounding skin surfaces by the bullet as it indents the skin before perforating it. Although this may in some cases be partially true, as when a bullet strikes the skin at a low angle of incidence, such an explanation is incomplete. High-speed photography has demonstrated that in the case of a bullet striking at an angle perpendicular to the body, the so-called "abrasion" ring is actually more plausibly produced by the overstretching of the skin during the production of the temporary cavity; direct contact by the bullet does not appear to occur at the margin of the wound but rather occurs only in the most central portion of the affected region of skin, which is shredded and expelled in the process.
If the bullet strikes the skin at an angle other than perpendicular, the abrasion will be widest along the margin from whence the bullet came (toward the muzzle of the firearm). Assuming a flat skin surface, this may provide a clue as to the path of the bullet; however, any curvature, indentation, or protrusion of the body surface will complicate this analysis (see the following images).
Not all entrance defects conform to these typical features; thus, the distinction of entrance vs exit wounds is not always a simple matter. With the exception of findings pathognomonic for contact and intermediate-range wounds, no single feature of a firearm wound of entrance (e.g., shape, abrasion, size) is necessarily diagnostic of an entrance wound. Rather, the totality of the wound features should be considered to differentiate between entrance and exit wounds.
Where uncertainty exists due to the potential morphological overlap between entrance and exit wounds, the pathologist may best be served by identifying which defects are linked by wound tracks (after comprehensive documentation and photography of all wounds has been completed) before finalizing an opinion regarding entrances and exits. As opposed to an attempt to classify potentially numerous wounds as entrances or exits based on external examination alone, this practice will present the pathologist with a series of binary decisions between only two alternatives, one of which must perforce be an entrance, and the other an exit.
Finally, it should also be noted that the caliber of an impacting projectile cannot be reliably determined from the size of an entrance wound on the skin.

### Distant/indeterminate wounds

If the muzzle of the firearm is more than a few feet from the skin, there will be no residues of any kind deposited on the body surrounding the firearm wound. This type of wound is classified as a distant wound. Some practitioners refer to these as "indeterminate" wounds, based on the concept that one cannot entirely rule out the possibility that the wound was produced by firing at close range through an intermediate target that screened the firearm residues from the skin. This should, of course, be considered as a possibility during the examination of death scenes and the body of a firearm wound victim.

### Hard contact wounds, stellate wounds, and muzzle imprints

The discharge of copious amounts of hot gas, soot, unburned or partially burnt powder particles, metallic fragments, or other debris from the muzzle of a firearm, along with the bullet, may result in characteristic findings on the skin or clothing of a firearm wound victim. When the muzzle of a firearm is held firmly against the skin of a victim as it is fired, a hard contact wound results. In this wound, a dark zone of soot (carbon) deposition is present around the edge of the entrance wound, as shown in the image below. This soot is seared into the skin by hot gases exiting the firearm muzzle and cannot be completely washed away from the wound. It may also be possible to discern a cherry red color of the underlying soft tissues due to the carboxyhemoglobin formed when the carbon monoxide that is formed from the burning powder charge is injected into the tissues with the muzzle blast.
If the muzzle of a firearm is held firmly against the skin overlying a flat bony surface, such as the skull, a stellate wound may result. This irregular wound may be quite large and results from gases expelled from the firearm dissecting beneath the skin of the wound, with resultant explosive rupture of the skin as the gas expands. Although such a large and irregular wound is often misinterpreted by untrained individuals as an exit wound, the large quantities of soot often present within the wound and on the underlying skull should be a clue to its true nature (see the following image).
The same process of gas expansion beneath the skin may result in a muzzle imprint. In a contact firearm wound, gas may dissect beneath the skin and cause it to expand or "balloon" outward, forcibly impacting the muzzle of the impinging firearm. This results in an impact abrasion that may partially or fully replicate the configuration of the firearm muzzle (see the first image below). The actual wound defect may also have a stellate appearance if it is over the skull (see the second image below).
Another contact firearm wound phenomenon results in a radial strip of soot and seared skin that extends from the edge of a firearm wound. Various explanations for the phenomenon have been offered, but it may result when, after the bullet enters the body, the muzzle of the gun slides across the skin for a brief instant as hot gas and soot continue to issue from the muzzle. This may result in "painting" a strip of soot and/or searing radially from the wound (see the following image).
Another explanation for this phenomenon of a radial strip of soot and radially extending seared skin is that it represents an incomplete contact wound, resulting from a partial break in contact between the muzzle of the firearm and the skin, allowing a jet of hot gas to extend outward from the wound. However, in most wounds, the area of soot deposition and burning might be expected to be fan-shaped as opposed to bandlike. This process appears to be particularly common with .22 caliber rimfire rifles, but it is also noted with larger-caliber handguns.
Muzzle compensators or muzzle brakes may also produce characteristic findings on the skin or clothing around a wound. These are devices on the muzzle of a firearm that direct gas upward and perpendicularly (usually) from the barrel of the gun to help limit muzzle rise and attenuate recoil. Such a compensator may consist of two slots milled into the barrel and/or slide of a pistol or other firearm, allowing gas to issue in a radial fashion from each side of the upper edge of the muzzle end of the firearm on firing. This may result in marking (with searing and/or soot deposition) the skin around the wound in a "V" shape. Similar marks may be seen from flash suppressors on rifle or shotgun barrels. In the case of flash suppressors, the radial burns may extend from multiple points around the wound, depending on the type of suppressor installed (see the images below).

### Loose -contact /near-contact wounds

When the barrel of a firearm is held in loose contact with the skin or up to a few centimeters away, soot may be deposited loosely on the skin (see the following image). The soot from this loose-contact or near-contact wound is not seared in place like that of a hard contact wound, and it may be largely washed away during cleaning of blood from the wound. Consequently, examination and photographic documentation should take place before cleaning. Once this has been done, gentle cleaning of the wound with warm water and very light scrubbing may serve to remove obscuring blood while leaving the majority of the soot on the skin. In either case, the overall pattern of soot distribution and its area should be documented. Keep in mind that some modern ammunition is quite "clean," with much less soot and powder deposition than might be expected.

### Bullet wipe

Take care not to confuse soot surrounding a close-range (contact or intermediate) firearm wound with bullet wipe. As a bullet passes though the barrel of a firearm, it picks up a coating of powder, soot, lead, dirt, oil, or any other material soiling the bore of the gun. This may be transferred to or "wiped" on the edges of the bullet defect on the skin or clothing of the victim, presenting as a gray, thin rim of discoloration at the margin of the wound (see the image below). It may be seen with either close-range or distant wounds and is not related to the range of fire.

### Intermediate-range wounds and gunpowder stippling/tattooing

As a muzzle is moved farther away from the victim's skin, beyond contact or loose-contact range, the phenomenon of gunpowder stippling, also commonly known as powder tattooing, may occur. This is caused by the impact of partially burnt or unburned gunpowder particles on the victim's skin, causing tiny abrasions. The presence of stippling/tattooing indicates and defines an intermediate-range wound. In some cases, the particles of powder may be embedded in the abrasions. Such areas may be examined under magnification to determine the shape and size of marks, as well as the presence or absence of powder grains.
Because various types of gunpowder have different shapes (e.g., ball, flattened ball, flake, cylindrical), stipple marks may also have correspondingly different shapes. In the case of handguns, stippling/tattooing is typically seen with a muzzle-to-target distance range from about 1 cm to 1 m or less. By measuring the dimensions of the area of stippling on a victim's skin and comparing it with that found on test firing a suspect weapon at varying known ranges, an estimation of muzzle-to-target distance may be determined.
When measuring the extent of stippling, one should measure the diameter of the primary mass of stipple marks, without including the occasional outlier. The distribution of the stippling around the wound should also be noted; for example, equal over all quadrants surrounding the wound vs more prominent over some quadrants (see the following image).
If a firearm muzzle is close enough to the victim, both soot deposition and stippling may occur, as shown in the image below. This generally indicates a narrow range of fire, with a muzzle-to-target distance of a few centimeters.
Many phenomena may mimic true stippling or powder tattooing (pseudostippling). Impact abrasions with dirt or gravel, insect feeding marks, bullet splatter (caused by a bullet impacting a hard surface near the body and extensively fragmenting), needle suture marks, and tiny abrasions caused by postmortem shaving are but a few things that may cause confusion. However, true gunpowder stippling marks are usually fairly similar in size and shape, as well as evenly distributed. Pseudostippling marks are often quite variable in size and configuration (see the following image).
If the muzzle of a gun is held close to the skin, at an acute angle to the body, an angled firearm entrance wound may result. In such cases, the deposition of soot and/or powder stippling at different points around the wound will vary asymmetrically according to the angle of the trajectory and the muzzle-to-target distance. Space does not allow for description of the many variants of this type of wound here, so readers are referred to the excellent discussion of this topic in DiMaio's textbook. However, the zones of soot deposition or powder stippling will generally be denser on the side of the wound adjacent to the muzzle, and they will be broader and less dense opposite the muzzle of the firearm (see the image below).
Recall that gunpowder stippling/tattooing, by definition, encompasses the small abrasions created by gunpowder particles striking and sometimes becoming embedded in the skin. Because gunpowder particles have relatively low mass, they rapidly lose the velocity necessary to create powder tattooing, accounting for the relatively close-range firing distances noted above. However, individual particles of gunpowder may still travel beyond that range and be deposited upon the body or clothing, without embedding into or abrading the skin. Compared to the range of fire productive of stippling, deposition of gunpowder particles may occur at distances 2-5 times greater.
Clothing or other coverings may partially or completely screen out soot or gunpowder stippling from the body. Therefore, clothing overlying a firearm wound should always be evaluated directly by the forensic pathologist and submitted to more extensive criminalistic testing for firearm residues if the range of fire is a critical issue.

### Graze wounds

The trajectory of a projectile may be tangential to the skin surface. In such a case, a bullet may skim across the skin, disrupting the superficial layers of the skin and subcutis, producing a graze type wound of the skin. It is often difficult to determine the directionality of a graze wound, but occasionally, a well-formed abrasion will be present at one end of the graze wound, indicating the site of initial contact by the bullet. Sometimes, the tearing of the skin by the passage of the bullet results in skin tags that protrude into the wound. Generally, these tags will point toward the direction from whence the bullet came. If this finding is present and consistent, an opinion regarding the path of a bullet may be formed (see the following images).

### Atypical entrance wounds, intermediary target effects, re-entrant wounds

The typical round-to-oval shape of a firearm entrance wound results from a stabilized, rapidly rotating bullet impacting and perforating the skin nose first. If the bullet has been deformed or destabilized, an atypical entrance wound may result (see the image below). Such an atypical wound will have an irregular shape with irregular abrasion at the margin.
A bullet may be destabilized by striking a so-called intermediary or interposed target. This may be any structure that the bullet perforates before striking the victim. Automobile doors or glass, house doors or windows, and pillows are common intermediary targets. When this occurs, the bullet may yaw instead of proceeding in a stabilized, nose-forward path. This may cause the bullet to strike the victim sideways or base first. The bullet may also become deformed and misshapen, and it may break up, with fragments of the bullet and/or the intermediary target being driven into the body. The resulting wound(s) is (are) large and irregular, and smaller satellite wounds resulting from impacts by the multiple fragments may also be seen on the surrounding skin. When a bullet ricochets off a hard surface before striking a body, it will likely deform, so a similar atypical wound may be produced (see the following image).
A bullet may also pass through one body part, usually an arm or leg, before entering another part of the body, such as the trunk. The wound on the trunk is referred to as a re-entrant or re-entrance wound, and it may be irregular, with characteristics of an atypical firearm wound. If the two body parts are in close opposition when the wound is received, both the exit wound and the re-entrant wound may show contusion and/or abrasion (see the images below).

### Exit wounds

Exit wounds are usually irregular, have no abrasion rim, and do not display soot deposition or stippling. They may have a stellate configuration or, particularly in the event of a low-velocity exit wound, have a slit-like configuration. These can be deceptively sharp appearing and can mimic a stab wound (see the following images).
A common misconception among untrained individuals is that exit wounds are always larger than entrance wounds. However, this is often incorrect, particularly in the case of contact wounds of the head. As noted earlier, such entrance wounds are often large and stellate, whereas the corresponding exit wound may be quite small. The protrusion of fat or viscera through a wound has also been misinterpreted to indicate that the wound is an exit wound. In reality, fat or viscera may herniate from a wound due to the effects of gravity or pressure, and this finding cannot be used to determine entrance vs exit wounds.
A shored exit wound results when the skin at the point of bullet exit is supported or buttressed by some object causing abrasion around the exit wound. The object may be close-fitted or heavy clothing, the floor beneath the victim, a chair back if seated, or any other item that presses against the exit wound area. In such a case, the skin at the exit point is tented outward by the exiting bullet and driven into the supporting or shoring material, causing abrasion, contusion, and/or laceration of the skin around the exit wound. The abrasion may be patterned, recapitulating the shape and character of the overlying "shoring" material. In some such cases, the bullet will fail to exit the skin and will be found in the subcutis or dermis, while causing abrasion or contusion of the overlying skin (see the images below).

### Palmar or plantar wounds

Entrance firearm wounds on the palms or soles are often not typical, being irregular and devoid of abrasion rims (see the following image). Stippling may not be easily seen on wounds of these areas, and marginal tears may also be present at the wound edges.
One feature that may aid determination of an entrance wound in the palm or sole is the presence of a relatively concentric and well-defined rim of erythema surrounding the wound, even when the defect itself is stellate. This erythematous rim is often absent in exit defects. However, determination as to whether a wound of the palm or sole represents an entrance vs an exit wound must often be determined by factors other than wound morphology.

### Beveling

When a bullet strikes a flat bone, such as the skull, the phenomenon of beveling may occur. When the bullet perforates the bone, the surface opposite that which was initially struck is fractured away, surrounding the bullet perforation. The beveled surface has a cone-like shape, with the apex of the cone pointing toward the muzzle of the firearm. Hence, the entrance defect a bullet makes in the skull will have beveling of the inner table of the cranium (internal beveling) (see the first image below). The exit defect will display external beveling (see the second and third images below).
Such beveling may be very helpful in determining the path of a bullet if the wounds of the skin are indeterminate, altered, or absent (in decomposed or skeletonized bodies, for example). Although beveling is most commonly encountered in the skull, it may also be quite helpful when involving the ribs or other flat bones. Even if beveling is not present, extrusion of splinters of bone along the wound path may also occur and is indicative of the path of bullet travel.

### Keyhole wounds

Keyhole wounds occur when a bullet strikes the skull tangentially, causing a single, elongated defect that demonstrates characteristics of both an entrance wound (internal beveling) at the point the bullet initially strikes the skull and characteristics of an exit wound (external beveling) distal to this. This results in a defect reminiscent of an old skeleton keyhole. The bullet may completely exit the skull through the keyhole defect, it may enter the skull, or it may fragment, with a portion remaining in the skull while another portion exits. The direction of travel is easily discerned from a well-formed keyhole defect because it proceeds from the internally beveled end toward the externally beveled terminus (see the following image).

### Intraoral wounds

Intraoral firearm wounds are commonly seen with self-inflicted (suicidal) firearm wounds. Like other types of firearm wounds, they may demonstrate soot or stippling on the mucosa of the palate or pharynx (see the image below). Stretch lacerations of the lips may occur with intraoral firearm wounds if the ammunition is sufficiently powerful, a result of the rapid and forceful expansion of gas issuing into the oral cavity. Extensive facial fracturing may also result.

### Blood spatter and soot deposition

Contact firearm wounds typically cause spattered blood to be deposited on the firearm and, often, the hand or body of the shooter (see the following image). Such spatter may take place in wounds other than contact wounds, with its degree decreasing with the muzzle-to-target distance.
Soot may also be deposited on the hands of a shooter or a victim at close range. This soot may issue from the muzzle of the firearm or from the cylinder gap of a revolver. In self-inflicted wounds, the grip of a firearm is often held with one hand, while the opposite hand grips the barrel of the weapon near the muzzle to steady it against the body. In such a case, it is common for soot from the muzzle to be deposited on the dorsal web of the thumb and the adjacent lateral aspect of the index finger of the steadying (non-firing) hand. Other residues, such as rust, may be left on a hand in prolonged contact with a metal firearm, and occasionally, an imprint of a handgun grip may be left on the palm of a hand in which a gun was held (see the images below).
Because hot gases and residues may issue forcibly from the cylinder gap of a discharging revolver, telltale findings may accompany the improper firing of such a weapon. In a self-inflicted firearm wound from a revolver, in which the firearm is held backward and gripped by two hands, gunpowder stippling may be found on the ventral forearms due to powder grains issuing from the cylinder gap. If one hand is held over the cylinder area of a powerful revolver, such as a .357 or .44 Magnum, the blast from the cylinder gap may be forceful enough to cause significant laceration of the overlying hand (see the following image).

## Shotgun Wounds

Shotgun wounds are unique by virtue of the fact that the ammunition (referred to as a shotshell) usually contains multiple, small projectiles (shot) instead of a single bullet. However, in a contact shotgun wound, the shot charge enters the body en masse , causing a wound that resembles that of a single projectile. In such a case, there may be a round-to-oval wound with marginal abrasion. Due to the long barrels typical of shotguns, combustion of the powder charge may be more complete, such that soot and powder stippling may be less apparent than in wounds seen with handguns.
Once a shotshell has been fired, the mass of pellets gradually begins to expand and separate as distance from the muzzle increases; this allows assessment of the range of fire in most shotgun wounds.
As the muzzle-to-target distance increases from a contact wound, a more irregular entrance wound with scalloped edges may occur from early pellet spread. As distance increases to a few meters, the margin of the wound will become ragged; at a slightly longer distance, discrete individual "satellite" defects may be seen around the main wound due to individual pellet strikes (see the following images).
Beyond this point, as range increases, the main perforation becomes progressively less defined, and individual pellet defects are more widely distributed. The precise rate of this pellet spread is highly variable, depending on factors such as type of firearm, shell construction, and choke (muzzle constriction) of the shotgun. When determining the range of fire by the degree of pellet spread, only a very rough approximation may be made, unless the actual firearm and ammunition are available for test firing.
Stippling may also occur around shotgun wounds. The stippling may be due to powder particles, as in handgun wounds, but a similar phenomenon may be seen in shotgun wounds inflicted by buckshot rounds. Buckshot shells may contain a granulated plastic buffer admixed with the shot pellets to cushion them when the round discharges. Upon firing, the granules of filler may produce stippling of the skin at a greater range (up to 3 m) than powder particles (see the image below).
At distant ranges, pellet spread may be such that no main pellet mass perforation is present, and the individual pellet wounds appear evenly distributed (see the following image).
The projectiles (shot) of most modern shotgun shells are contained in a plastic cup, with slit sides forming petals that allow the cup to open as it leaves the barrel. On exiting the muzzle, the cup is intact, and it contains the shot as a single mass. Due to aerodynamic pressure, the petals of the cup rapidly begin to open and extend once the cup has left the muzzle; after a few feet, the petals are completely folded back. At short ranges, the skin around the wound may be marked or abraded by the extended petals of the cup (see the image below).
At slightly longer distances, the completely folded petals may not mark the skin as the shot charge and cup enter the skin together through a single defect. At still longer distances, the cup will fall away from the shot charge and may abrade the skin separately at some distance from the main shot mass. At distances beyond about 20 feet, no marking of the skin by the shot cup will usually be seen. Most modern shot cups have four petals when fully extended, although the smallest shotshell, the .410, usually has only three petals.
Target shotshells, buckshot rounds, or older ammunition may contain no shot cup. Instead, a series of cardboard, plastic, or felt discs (wadding) may be placed over the powder charge, beneath the pellets. These may also mark the skin at close ranges (see the following image).
All wadding or shot cups should be recovered from the body during autopsy examination, as their characteristics may provide insight into the type of ammunition used. Their presence within the body also has bearing on the range of fire. Because wadding or shot cups are difficult or impossible to visualize on radiographs, a careful manual and visual search of the wound track is indicated to find them in appropriate cases.
Another projectile that may be fired in a shotgun is the slug, which consists of a single, large-bore projectile, usually lead. The most common type of slug is the Foster slug, composed of lead with a hollow base and diagonal "vanes" milled or cut into its surface to stabilize the projectile. The Foster slug produces a large entrance wound and does not usually exit the body; however, it does often fragment into a few large pieces. Some other types of slugs are less common in the United States, including sabot and Brenneke rounds. The sabot round has a projectile smaller than the shotgun bore diameter, enclosed in a plastic casing or "sabot" that falls away as the unit exits the muzzle of the gun. At close ranges, this sabot may mark the skin around the wound. The Brenneke round consists of a lead projectile with a wad that is actually screwed to its base; as such, this round has a fairly distinct appearance on radiographs.
Finally, shotgun ammunition can be loaded with a combination of a slug and buckshot pellets, as a modern incarnation of historical "buck-and-ball" loads sometimes utilized in smoothbore, muzzle-loading muskets. Depending upon the range of fire, wounds produced by such ammunition can show a characteristic pattern of a large central defect (corresponding to the passage of the slug) flanked by adjacent, nearly equidistantly spaced smaller satellite defects from the pellets. See the figure below.
Contact and intraoral shotgun wounds of the head may cause explosive damage of the skull, rendering the identification of entrance and exit points difficult. However, reapproximation of wound edges and bones will often show areas of skin abrasion or soot deposition to indicate the site of the entrance wound. Gray lead markings on the skull caused by shot pellet strikes may be seen, assisting in the determination of the trajectory of the shotgun blast (see the image below).

## Centerfire Rifle Wounds

Wounds from centerfire rifles differ from those made by handguns, primarily because of the much higher velocity and kinetic energy of the projectiles. Recall that the kinetic energy of a projectile increases as the mass and especially the velocity increase, according to the formula KE = 1/2 (MV 2 ), where KE = kinetic energy, M = mass, and V = velocity.
Rifle entrance wounds are typically regular and sharply punched out if no intermediary target or other destabilizing influence is applied to the bullet, and they may, in the case of smaller caliber projectiles, present a deceptively innocuous appearance. Wounds due to 5.56 × 45 mm cartridges will sometimes have a hexagonal abrasion rim; however, in many cases, abrasion rims may be thin to absent, and there may be tiny radial "microtears" at the margin of the wound (see the images below).
Because of the high velocity associated with centerfire rifle wounds, bullets often fragment extensively within the body, resulting in the so-called "lead snowstorm" appearance on radiographs. As the muzzle-to-target distance increases, bullet velocity and energy will decrease, and so will fragmentation. Although it will usually be less extensive in long-distance wounds, significant bullet breakup is still the rule, unless the bullet is heavily constructed (see the following images).
When a bullet passes through tissue, it crushes tissue, resulting in a permanent cavity. It also causes a short-lived temporary cavity, the size and shape of which are greatly influenced by the velocity of the bullet. Because this cavity is partially dependent on velocity, it is much more prominent in centerfire rifle wounds than those due to handguns. The deformation of tissue due to this cavity may cause significant stretching and tearing of the skin and soft tissues of the injured area, the severity of which is partially determined by the elasticity and resilience of the injured organs (see the images below). If a bone is struck and fragmented, the tissue damage will be amplified by bone fragments driven along the wound track by the bullet.
Contact rifle wounds show characteristics similar to handgun wounds, although, like shotgun wounds or very powerful handgun wounds, they may have explosive effects when applied to the head. Similar to that of long-barreled shotguns, soot and stippling may be less prominent than that seen in short-barreled handguns. Even distant wounds of the head due to high-velocity rifles may be deceptively stellate because of temporary cavitation.
Projectiles commonly perforate (pass through) one area of the body, such as an arm or leg, before striking the head or torso. In such cases, the initial extremity wound causes destabilization of the bullet, often resulting in a large, atypical re-entry wound on the head or torso (see the following image). If the extremity is in contact with or closely approximated to the torso, there may be contusion and/or abrasion of the skin around the exit and re-entrant wounds.

## Common Misconceptions

Misconceptions among lay people and physicians abound regarding firearm wounds. For example, many physicians appear to believe that entrance firearm wounds are always smaller than exit wounds. This frequently causes confusion in cases of contact wounds of the head, which, as noted earlier, are often large and stellate (see the following image). Because the size of any firearm defect in the body is related to the amount of energy being transferred from the projectile to tissue at that particular point along the wound track, the relative sizes of entrance and exit defects can vary considerably. Thus, it is not uncommon for clinicians such as emergency department physicians or trauma surgeons to misinterpret the directionality of perforating wounds; therefore, it is generally preferable for such specialists to defer such interpretation to the forensic pathologist, at least in fatal cases.
Due to Hollywood portrayals of gunfights and firearm wounds, some people hold the erroneous opinion that a single firearm wound will cause immediate incapacitation in all cases and that a bullet will knock a victim to the ground, against a wall, or through a window. None of this is true.
Victims often sustain firearm wounds, sometimes multiple wounds, without becoming incapacitated. Even a belief that gunshot wounds to the head invariably result in immediate incapacitation is not universally accurate. Studies have documented cases where individuals sustained penetrating cranial gunshot wounds yet retained the capacity for purposeful movement, particularly when low-velocity projectiles were involved. Only more significant central nervous system injuries or wounds that compromises the stability and integrity of the supporting skeleton can generally be expected to cause a victim to stop and fall to the ground immediately. The kinetic energy of a bullet striking a target may appear fairly high (in the range of 500-1000 ft-lb of energy for a typical handgun cartridge), but because the bullet mass is so small, the actual momentum transferred to the target is quite small. As such, bullets from small arms do not throw or knock victims about. Simple Newtonian physics dictate that the recoil impulse of firing a projectile capable of hurling victims bodily upon impact would, of necessity, either throw the unsupported shooters a similar distance in the opposite direction or rip the firearms from their grasp.
Conversely, some cinematic presentations show firearm victims being rather innocuously wounded in locations such as the extremities or shoulder by a well-placed shot from a hero who "only wanted to 'wing' them, not kill them." In fact, wounds of the extremities are often fatal and/or maiming injuries, and in a gunfight, such precise shot placement on a moving target is highly improbable.
Although the effects of firearm wounds are often understood incorrectly, other facets of the subject are also improperly understood or reported by lay persons, medical personnel, or the media. Nowhere is this more evident than in discussions of the alleged use of "assault rifles" by criminal elements in the United States. In truth, assault rifles are fully automatic weapons that are quite difficult for civilians to obtain, and their criminal use is seen very rarely in most jurisdictions, if at all. Semiautomatic rifles that are commonly used by civilians (both the law-abiding and criminal elements) are generally not fully automatic in their function and thus have little in common with true assault rifles, other than their appearance. Such weapons are also actually not particularly powerful when compared with typical hunting rifles, as assault rifles were historically chambered for intermediate-power cartridges in order to allow the average soldier to control the weapon during fully automatic fire.
The physician is well advised to view all statements about firearms from media or other similar sources with some suspicion until they are corroborated by an expert in the field.

## Self-Inflicted (Suicidal) Firearm Wounds

Like firearm wounds in general, some myths regarding suicidal wounds persist. Handguns are the preferred method for suicide in many areas, and the demographics of such wounds indicate that they will be seen in all racial and ethnic groups as well as both sexes. A wide age range is also represented in adults.
Suicide notes are found in a minority of suicidal deaths due to firearm wounds, just as in general suicide deaths. The wounds are most common on the head, but in a significant number of cases, they are on the side of the head opposite that of the dominant hand. Because of this, "handedness" is not of much use in determining if a wound was self-inflicted or not. Most are intraoral (see the image below) or on the temporoparietal regions, but finding wounds on the posterior reaches of the head or neck is not particularly uncommon.
The demographics, location, and characteristics of a wound may give some indication of the statistical likelihood of the suicidal nature of the injury, and many authors have addressed this issue. Such information, however, does not provide definitive indication or refutation of a suicidal manner of death.
Morphologically, suicidal wounds should generally be either contact or intermediate range, unless an intermediary structure such as a pillow has been interposed between the body and the muzzle of the weapon; thick hair may also result in a more indeterminate-range wound appearance. However, some rather ingenious devices have been rigged by individuals intent on taking their own lives while attempting to camouflage the event as a homicide. A thorough scene investigation is imperative, as it is rare for examination of the body alone to indicate that a firearm wound is suicidal. As in so many types of cases examined by the forensic pathologist, the autopsy on its own does not give the manner of death in suicidal firearm wounds; correlation of the autopsy findings with the information found during the remainder of the investigation is of paramount importance.
It is also important to document the location of a firearm relative to the body in a putative suicidal firearm wound case. Although a dropped weapon may be found up to a few feet from the body, finding a firearm a significant distance away from the subject of an incapacitating wound would cause one to reconsider the possibility of a suicide. In the case of a wound that would not be immediately incapacitating, however, the firearm might be found a considerable distance away from the point of death, as the victim may have walked some distance after sustaining the wound.
Along the same lines, multiple firearm wounds in suicides are not uncommon. In some cases, an initial firearm wound may not prove fatal. This is seen sometimes when a handgun is placed beneath the chin, but the head is tilted too far backward, resulting in a wound track through the floor of the mouth, maxilla, and nasal region. Although serious and potentially fatal, this type of wound might not immediately kill or incapacitate, causing the subject to fire a second round with greater effect. Wounds to the trunk are often not immediately incapacitating, allowing ample time for the decedent to fire multiple times.
In the case of long-barreled weapons, consideration must be given to the location of the wound and whether it would have been possible for the decedent to position the weapon and manipulate the trigger of the firearm to inflict the wound. Remember that feet and toes, sticks, strings, or other devices may be utilized to activate a trigger, so the scene must be thoroughly examined.
Finally, there is great variability in the classification of self-inflicted firearm wounds as "suicide" among forensic pathologists. Some consider that intent to die is required to classify a death as a suicide. Others counter that absolute proof of intent to die is not required to yield a suicidal manner of death, arguing instead that since placing a weapon against one's body and pulling the trigger violates all rules of gun safety, this action in and of itself indicates such willingness to accept a possible lethal outcome that it merits a suicidal classification. A prime example is a death due to "Russian roulette." Many consider such deaths to be suicidal, whereas others consider them to be impulsive acts, often facilitated by ethanol or other intoxicants, and not indicative of a self-destructive intent.
Similar quandaries arise in cases when a firearm that is "thought" to be unloaded is placed against the head and fired, resulting in the death of the shooter. In cases of deaths caused by another person, a similar quandary exists. When a person is mistaken for a game animal and is inadvertently killed by the shot of a hunter, is it a homicide or an accident? Although the hunter may not have intended to kill the victim, the firearm was willfully discharged at a target, albeit erroneously.
These inconsistencies indicate a general lack of agreement on the definitions of various manners of death among forensic pathologists as well as variation in the philosophical approaches to manner of death determination. In any case, most forensic pathologists will agree that truly accidental firearm wounds are rare.

## Issues Arising in Court

The imagination of trial attorneys when questioning an expert witness will often amaze even experienced forensic pathologists, thus, the range of issues that the physician will be expected to address in court is practically infinite. However, certain questions arise in a high percentage of firearm wound cases and are briefly discussed below.
The pathologist is expected to give the cause of death in the case. Although this may seem obvious, remember that in a court of law, until evidence is actually given in the courtroom, it cannot be considered by the jury. Thus, testimony on such apparently obvious information will usually be required. Sometimes, little more will be asked of the forensic pathologist.
If there are multiple wounds, questioning may focus on which wounds were fatal and which were survivable. In responding, the witness should remember that forensic pathologists often have a skewed view of survivability, as their "patient" population is composed exclusively of nonsurvivors. In some cases, it may be prudent to defer such judgments to practitioners of other specialties, such as trauma surgeons. If one chooses to address such a question when asked, keep in mind that some victims can and do survive even devastating injuries, whereas others die from wounds that would often be survivable. In some cases, the answer to both the questions, "Could this wound be fatal?" and "Could this wound be survivable?" is "Yes." As questions regarding the potential lethality of wounds tend to revolve around the physician's ability to envision a plausible physiological scenario in which the wounds caused death, it can be seen that almost any wound could prove fatal, albeit by diverse mechanisms (e.g., direct trauma to the nervous system or heart; exsanguination; pulmonary thromboembolism complicating immobility following the injuries; infection of the wounds leading to sepsis; bowel obstruction by adhesions from abdominal wounds).
Conversely, answering questions regarding the potential survivability of wounds often requires consideration not only of the characteristics of the wounds themselves but also of the current state of medical advancements in trauma treatment and the accessibility of medical care to the victim. As an example of the former issue, students of history will recall that abdominal wounds injuring the bowels and productive of peritonitis were almost invariably fatal in the pre-anesthesia/pre-antibiotic era; by the 1980s, the survival of patients who presented to a major urban trauma center with abdominal gunshot wounds causing visceral or vascular injuries was nearly 90%. Regarding the latter issue, the pathologist might express very different opinions regarding the survivability of a gunshot wound to the lower extremity that fractures the femur and injures the femoral vasculature, depending upon if it was inflicted a few city blocks from a trauma center or during a hunting trip in a remote mountainous region.
The range of fire is often questioned, particularly if one side of the dispute alleges that a struggle took place at close range at the time of the shooting. Characteristics of the firearm wound may assist in this determination, but the presence of overlying clothing must be considered. If present, the clothing should be examined in a crime laboratory to properly assess the degree of soot and/or powder deposition present. Only a gross estimate of range of fire may be given (for instance, "inches," "up to a few feet," "more than a few feet") unless the firearm and ammunition used in the shooting have been recovered and test-fired for a more accurate range of fire determination.
Incapacitation due to a firearm wound will often come into question. As noted elsewhere in this article, only wounds that significantly injure the upper central nervous system might be reasonably expected to cause immediate incapacitation, whereas those that cause significant fractures of the supporting skeletal elements may have deleterious effects on the movement of the victim. Yet even victims with devastating injuries may continue to function for a period, and this may involve fleeing, continuing an assault, running, or other activities. The period in which this may occur is highly variable according to the degree of injury and individual personal factors that are difficult to quantitate.
One of the more common queries in court relates to the trajectory of the bullet striking a victim. The forensic pathologist determines an approximate path of the bullet through the body (based on the standard anatomic position) but not through space. As the human body can move and assume a great variety of positions, the actual trajectory of the bullet through space will be dependent on the position of the body when the wound is inflicted. This is rarely known, but it is common for opposing attorneys to offer hypothetical scenarios on the position of a body, asking the pathologist if these scenarios are consistent with the wounds found. The question is perfectly legitimate, and it should be answered honestly to the effect that the postulated position is "consistent" but is not the only possible position. Unfortunately, the multitude of possibilities in such cases provides ample opportunities for opposing attorneys to attempt to gain ground in the courtroom, so prolonged questioning about positioning and bullet paths can be expected in some cases.
The pathologist is sometimes asked to identify a bullet removed at autopsy in court. Because it is practically impossible to visually identify a bullet that was likely removed years earlier, the best response is that the bullet can be identified by the labeling on the container in which it was placed, and has been handled with a strict chain of custody throughout its examination. It is up to the prosecuting attorney to demonstrate that the chain of custody was maintained. Opening containers and removing bullets and other evidence for examination in court is generally poor practice, although it is often requested that the witness do so as part of the "theater" of the courtroom. If told to do so, there is no recourse, except to request a pair of gloves with which to handle the contaminated evidence.
The forensic pathologist must attempt to answer all questions accurately and honestly, even if it is difficult to determine why the question is being asked. If the question is confusing or difficult to understand, the pathologist should not hesitate to say so and to ask for clarification. If the answer requires explanation or expository statements, the pathologist should attempt to give them and, if there is an objection, should simply state that the question cannot be answered without explanation. Most judges will allow an expert witness to explain an answer rather than being confined to a simple "yes/no" choice of answers. If the answer to a question is not known, the pathologist should simply say so. It is a truism in forensic pathology that the willingness to say "I do not know" is a measure of the maturity and experience of the practitioner.
